I watched [DHH's Rails World 2026 opening keynote](https://youtu.be/vDjW_dRyKXY) and it's the talk about this moment I've enjoyed most. Writing down what stuck, because a lot of it did.

## Optimism, on purpose

DHH spends the keynote pushing optimism instead of pessimism, and I think that's the only thing that makes sense. Obviously we should stay cautious — security is real, nobody actually knows what the economy looks like after a shift like this — but pessimism buys you nothing. If the future turns out fine, you spent your time scared for nothing. If it doesn't, you should have spent your last day a little more cheerful. He says it as a game-theory joke, but I buy the real argument: with predictions this unreliable, optimism is the rational default, not a personality type.

## Everyone gets a camera, art gets interesting

The analogy I loved: a portrait in the 18th century meant sitting for months in front of a master painter, and only royalty could afford one. Then the camera, then the Kodak Brownie at roughly a dollar a picture, and suddenly everyone could get their picture taken. Two trillion photos a year now.

The part that lands for me is what happened to art. Realism — reproducing exactly what's in front of you — stopped being a viable job the moment a machine could do it cheaper. And artists didn't disappear. They pivoted. Picasso went cubist, the impressionists chased light instead of detail. Art got less realistic and more interesting precisely because the realistic part got automated.

That's the shape of this. The camera didn't kill art; it freed it from the part the camera does better.

## Bigger than the internet

He positions this as bigger than computers, bigger than the internet — the intelligence revolution — and I think that framing is right. I lived through the internet arriving, but young enough that it was just *there*. This is the first time I can feel the before-and-after while it's happening: everything before November 24th, everything after. His Kodak Brownie moment.

## The pace isn't slowing down

The argument I keep seeing — models will hit a wall, there's a ceiling to how good this can get — is the one I believe least. If anything it's the opposite. Releases went from once a month, once every two months, to weekly. A week where none of the big labs ship anything is a *silent* week now, and those feel weird.

Open weights especially: DeepSeek 4.1 Flash, MiMo v2.6 with its transparent RL runs, Muse from Meta catching up on a lot. None of these were close to this good three or four months ago. I got my own taste of frontier intelligence using Opus 5 recently, and Opus 5.5 is even better — so I'm positive about where that line goes. Whatever the labs have internally is probably the generation we'll see in the next two or three months. The public models are unbelievably good compared to a few months ago, and the ones behind closed doors are ahead of those. Very bullish.

## Look at all the things I'm not doing

The clip he plays of himself from Rails 2005 — "look at all the things I'm not doing" — is the best encapsulation of the moment I've heard. Not the code you're writing, the configuration you're not writing, the six native apps a small team "couldn't" maintain that now just get built.

And I like how he frames the new job: give a task to an agent like you'd give it to a coworker, send them off, come back and review when something's ready. Not sitting in a chat window watching tokens scroll. You stop being the person who chisels the code and become the person steering the intelligence — a professional maker of things. That reads less like a threat and more like a promotion.

## Permission to treat it as a black box

This is the one that changed something in my head. The older guard always says you must review every line, check the AI-generated code, never trust it. And sure — for some things, yes. But DHH's version: he doesn't know any Rust and considers that a *feature*. He evaluates the Rust box from the outside, like any business owner who ever commissioned programmers to build something for them. Same phenomenon, different who-holds-the-responsibility.

I'd always felt slightly guilty skipping the diff. Now I don't. Judging the output instead of the implementation isn't laziness — it's the skill. Treating it as a black box isn't a compromise you settle for; in a lot of cases it's the advantage.

## My own flip

DHH talks about retiring from being a professional programmer without regret, and that's the change I've felt in myself over the last few months. A while ago, if you'd asked me if I'm a vibe coder, I'd have said yes and hesitated — a little ashamed, like you were judging me for it.

Today it's reversed. If someone tells me they write every line of their production code by hand, I file them as an oldie. Fine as art or a hobby — if it relaxes your nerves, enjoy it, I don't hate you for it. But it's not economically viable, and it isn't going to be. Just don't try to defend hand-writing every line as *the future*. DHH's phrasing is harsher than mine: we had a wonderful run chiseling code by hand, and it's over. Same conclusion.

## What I actually hope for

Caution where it counts, optimism everywhere else, and stop reading code you no longer need to. Beyond the arguments, that's what I want out of this: an age of abundance we've never seen before, and lives with more happiness than we can think of.
